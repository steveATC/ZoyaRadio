# K-DST production-pattern study for Zoya Radio

## Scope and result

This report analyzes the attached 66:41 K-DST recording as a piece of radio production. It does not reproduce scripts or attempt to transfer the station's characters, jokes, branding, advertisements, music, or other creative expression. Dialogue is summarized only at the level needed to classify production behavior.

The central measured result is that this recording uses the same broad **host-at-every-song-boundary** architecture found in the attached K-Rose reference, but it creates a substantially less talk-dense result. K-DST's host-led windows are shorter, more often contain dry voice rather than a continuous music bed, and much less often extend across an incoming intro. Its dominant grammar is closer to:

> outgoing tail or quick reset → compact persona/world material → optional stinger or caller → clean launch or selective intro talk-up

K-Rose's dominant grammar, by contrast, was a longer previous-song reaction and character detour that usually continued over the next intro. This comparison is empirical for the two supplied compilations; it is not proof of a universal rule for either station in live game assembly.

## Measurement method and confidence

### Direct measurements

- Source: `K-DST.mp3`, duration **66:41.256**, stereo MP3 at **32 kHz / 320 kb/s**.
- The file was decoded and measured for loudness, silence, stereo width, spectral balance, and 200 ms energy changes.
- Two local speech-recognition passes with word timing were used as navigation aids. Boundaries were then reconciled against acoustic changes, speaker changes, known song order, and the program sequence.
- Song identities were cross-checked against published K-DST track listings. External material was not used to derive timing, mixing, or behavioral statistics.
- The attached K-Rose report was used to preserve definitions and make the comparison like-for-like.
- The K-DST and K-Rose files carry the same compiler tag, sample rate, and bitrate. This makes their encoding broadly comparable, but also means both may reflect the same compilation method rather than an unedited in-game aircheck.

### Boundary confidence

- Host, caller, and imaging word boundaries: usually **±0.5 s**.
- Clear lyric and vocal boundaries: usually **±1 s**.
- Instrumental intro, fade, and mixed-section boundaries: generally **±2–3 s**; very quiet intros and interleaved outro sections are **±5 s**.
- Active-voice time is approximate because some voice blocks include breaths, pauses, FX, or music and because sung vocals can confuse speech recognition.
- All rates extrapolated per hour come from 66:41 of compiled material and should be treated as descriptive of this file, not as a stable station clock.

### Terms used below

- **Host-led window:** first host/imaging word through the last host/imaging word around a boundary, including deliberate gaps, callers, stingers, and music that occurs inside the window.
- **Active voice time:** approximate wall-clock time occupied by host, caller, or imaging speech. Because speech overlaps music, it is not additive with song airtime.
- **Produced stinger:** a prebuilt self-imaging unit with station voice, music, and/or FX. A host saying the station name inside an ordinary link is not automatically counted as a separate stinger.
- **Plain announcement:** artist/title identification with no joke, reaction, character material, world material, or imaging in the intervention.
- **Commercial:** a distinct paid-style or fictional-advertiser segment. Station self-imaging and listener calls are not commercials.

## Host behavior — measured observations

### Placement relative to music

- Every song boundary is host-mediated in this compilation, but only about **6/16 incoming launches** contain definite speech over the next track. One additional launch is marginal.
- The other approximately **10/16 launches are clean or near-clean**: the host and imaging finish before or essentially at music start.
- The opening is unusual: music begins, runs alone for about 8 s, and then receives a specific host talk-up.
- Among the definite incoming overlaps:
  - three begin while the host is already talking;
  - one begins quietly and the host re-enters about 13 s later;
  - one receives a host/ID re-entry about 2–3 s after music starts;
  - one long instrumental intro receives host speech within roughly 1–2 s and remains a bed for about 26 s.
- At least **nine post-song windows** contain unmistakable host speech over an outgoing tail. Several others begin on a final chord, very quiet fade, or stinger and cannot be separated confidently without source stems.
- No voice enters the established central verse or instrumental body of any song.

### What the host talks about

Conservative hand-coded relation counts:

- Defensible reference to the previous song, performer, title, year, or theme: **about 7/17 post-song links**.
- Discernible setup for the next song: **8/16 inter-song links**.
- Explicit next performer or title: **6/16 inter-song links**.
- Plain identification with no personality or world material: **0/18 windows**.
- Host autobiography, station mythology, fictional-world material, or attitude: present in **all 18 windows**.

The dominant semantic move is therefore different from K-Rose. K-DST often does not use the outgoing song as the engine of the whole link. It uses the boundary as permission to advance a persistent host persona: former-band history, rock-era nostalgia, station rivalry, lifestyle mythology, callers, or local-world commentary. Specific music identification is an occasional service element, usually placed at the end as a presell.

### Recurring themes and callbacks

Measured recurring devices include:

- Repeated fictional former-band and past-fame mythology.
- Repeated 1970s nostalgia and exaggerated rock-lifestyle material.
- Repeated authenticity tests built from clothing, genre, appearance, and behavior.
- Repeated ex-partner and relationship references.
- A recurring rivalry with another station/host.
- Repeated drugs, rehab, smoke, pollution, and bodily-wear motifs.
- Repeated “dust” wordplay integrated into station IDs.
- Listener calls that challenge the host's relevance, legacy, or station philosophy.

The callbacks are separated by several songs and are usually compressed into a single line or short cluster. They produce continuity without requiring a serialized plot.

### Restraint

Literal silence is rare, but K-DST shows strong behavioral restraint:

- Active voice occupies approximately **11.3%** of the file.
- All 17 central song bodies are left alone.
- Most incoming records launch cleanly rather than remaining beneath a long talk-up.
- The median uninterrupted no-host span is approximately **3:16**.
- Stingers usually replace or punctuate an already-open transition instead of creating an additional interruption.
- The 51-second maximum break is caused by a caller, not by an ordinary host monologue.

### Ducking and music-under-speech

The mix uses different rules for outgoing and incoming material.

- In nine sampled outgoing overlaps, side-channel music energy changes ranged from about **+1.5 to −12.2 dB** as the host entered, with a median fall of approximately **4.8 dB**.
- Across those same samples, total program RMS changed by a median of only about **−0.6 dB**, because the centered voice replaces much of the apparent level lost from the music.
- In six sampled incoming overlaps, side-channel energy during the final host portion was typically below the following clean music passage. The median difference was approximately **−4.7 dB**, but two quiet/long-intro arrangements produced 14 dB-class outliers.
- Total program RMS during those incoming host portions was about **1.0 dB higher** at the median than the immediately following music-only sample because voice adds centered energy.

The evidence supports moderate outgoing fade/duck behavior and more restrained or arrangement-dependent incoming beds. It does **not** support a single fixed attenuation value applied to every track. Without isolated stems, a quiet intro cannot be separated perfectly from deliberate ducking.

### Pauses, stops, fades, and overlap

- Several host and call sections are effectively dry: side-channel music energy falls close to the noise floor.
- Outgoing tracks commonly fade or remain beneath the first host line.
- Incoming songs more often start after the host than under the host.
- Two transitions deliberately let music begin and then bring the host back during the intro, rather than keeping speech continuous from the previous track.
- At several late-song boundaries, music continues or reappears between host phrases, creating an interleaved transition instead of a simple crossfade.
- There are no obvious hard digital clicks or long hard stops.
- The mixed master cannot establish whether an external music transport continues while a track is inaudible. No claim about hidden playback progression is warranted.

### Imaging, sonic logos, FX, and voices

- The eleven produced stinger placements use a small, repeated library rather than a unique sound on every transition.
- Sonic vocabulary centers on hard/classic-rock guitar or amp-like punctuation, compact spoken positioning lines, call-sign emphasis, and recurring dust/age imagery.
- The stingers are wider and spectrally brighter than the dry host, making them legible as produced station objects.
- The host voice is close, compressed, rough-edged, and strongly centered.
- Caller voices occupy a narrower, quieter, more band-limited acoustic space, immediately separating the fictional audience from the studio host.
- A separate produced imaging voice is perceptible in the stinger layer; it should not be treated as ordinary host delivery even when both are male voices.
- There is no commercial layer from which to measure ad announcers, ad beds, or ad-specific mastering.

## K-DST acoustic and sonic identity

### Observed

- Strongly centered, close male host voice against a generally wider classic-rock catalog.
- Loud, compressed overall master with only 4.7 LU of measured loudness range.
- A recurring library of rock-oriented stingers rather than one long sung jingle.
- More dry studio voice and clean song launches than in K-Rose.
- Moderate outgoing-tail attenuation while the voice keeps the total program near the same loudness plane.
- Telephone-like caller processing that is narrower, quieter, and more top-limited than the host.
- Station identity reinforced through repeated sonic motifs and wording families rather than neutral frequency reads.

### Interpretation

K-DST's sonic identity is built from **authority and separation**:

1. broad, energetic classic-rock records;
2. a close, rough, centered host voice;
3. short guitar/amp-like branded impacts;
4. drier spoken sections that allow the host's attitude to dominate briefly;
5. tightly filtered caller voices that sound outside the studio;
6. mostly clean launches that return control to the music.

The station feels personality-heavy without actually spending a large share of runtime on voice. Its confidence comes from consistent role treatment and recurring mythology, not from constant speech.

### Comparative interpretation

Both stations prove that “host after every song” does not describe perceived talk density well enough. K-Rose creates continuity by making the host semantically and acoustically bridge almost every pair of songs. K-DST creates continuity through repeated station attitude, stingers, and host mythology, then gives most incoming songs a cleaner entrance.

The useful design variables are therefore independent:

- whether a boundary receives any host material;
- how long the transition window remains open;
- how much of that window contains active voice;
- whether outgoing music remains audible;
- whether incoming music begins under speech;
- whether the link is song-specific, station-specific, or world-specific;
- whether imaging or a caller occupies part of the window.

Collapsing those into one “comment frequency” setting would throw away most of the production grammar.

## Potential lessons for Zoya Radio — transfer hypotheses

These are design lessons inferred from the measurements, not implementation decisions and not content to copy.

1. **Track boundary rate and voice occupancy separately.** K-DST and K-Rose touch every boundary, yet one devotes roughly half as much runtime to active voice. A scheduler needs both controls.
2. **Make incoming overlap a style parameter.** K-DST's mostly clean launches and K-Rose's near-universal talk-ups produce very different station identities without changing the song count.
3. **Use outgoing tails as lower-cost continuity.** Entering on a fade can keep the host present while preserving the next song's opening impact.
4. **Allow delayed talk-ups when an intro supports them.** Music can begin, breathe for a few seconds, and then receive a compact host line. This should be track-aware, not random.
5. **Do not require every link to be about the song.** Persistent host concerns and station mythology can create continuity. For Zoya, however, generic persona material must still be earned; this is not permission for irrelevant monologue spam.
6. **Treat clean launches as positive production, not as missing content.** Finishing the host before the next track is a deliberate musical choice.
7. **Use a small pre-rendered stinger library.** Ten distinct units reused across 66 minutes create a recognizable identity without live generation cost. Controlled repetition is doing useful work.
8. **Keep callers on their own clock.** Three calls account for all of the longest breaks. Caller rate and duration should not distort ordinary host-link targets.
9. **Separate performance from mix.** The host, callers, and imaging remain legible because they occupy different width, bandwidth, and loudness roles, not only because their words differ.
10. **Use pauses inside a prepared scene.** K-DST sometimes leaves several seconds between modules or allows music to reappear. These gaps feel intentional because the whole transition is composed; unplanned generation latency would not.
11. **Preserve the central song-body rule.** Both stations are aggressive at boundaries and disciplined inside songs. That remains the strongest transferable restraint principle.
12. **Maintain separate semantic modes.** Song reaction, factual announcement, host autobiography, station mythology, fictional-world material, caller scene, and imaging should not be flattened into one generic “comment.”
13. **Measure role-specific loudness and width.** A centered voice can remain intelligible without crushing every music bed by 10 dB or more.
14. **Do not infer that every song needs a host link.** Both supplied files are compilations of modular station material. Zoya Radio's music-first principle still permits multi-song silence and discarded opportunities.

## What should not be generalized from this station

- **The 16.2 windows/hour rate.** It comes from one compiled file, not a randomized multi-hour aircheck.
- **A host link after every song.** Both supplied files may have been assembled to include all available material.
- **The absence of commercials.** It means only that this file contains none.
- **The exact 10-stinger library or its reuse pattern.** Those are station-specific creative assets and may have been sequenced by the compiler.
- **The low rate of artist/title mentions.** It may be a deliberate trait of this host, not a universal fictional-radio rule.
- **The classic-rock intro lengths.** Other genres may provide less safe instrumental talk-up space.
- **The host's era, gender, vocal rasp, extremity, or subject matter.** Those are creative identity, not production law.
- **The apparent dynamic range and true peak.** The 32 kHz MP3 and source mastering affect those measurements.
- **The 17-song order.** San Andreas radio uses modular assembly; this is not evidence of a canonical repeating hour.
- **Caller frequency.** A compilation can concatenate an entire caller pool and exaggerate how often calls would occur in ordinary play.
- **The edited song lengths.** They are not full commercial-release durations and should not be used to plan a contemporary music service.
- **No mid-song speech as a universal prohibition.** It is a strong pattern in both supplied samples, but a third station or real broadcast may differ.

## Questions for the next station or capture

K-DST answers several of the K-Rose report's questions: host mediation is shared in these two compilations, but intro talk-up rate, active-voice share, semantic song relevance, silence count, and break length are not. The next useful test should target what remains unresolved.

1. Does an unedited in-game capture ever allow two- or three-song sweeps, or is boundary speech genuinely universal during play?
2. Do two separate captures of K-DST produce different song, host-line, call, and stinger orders, confirming the practical modular rules?
3. Which station includes a representative commercial pod, and how are ads scheduled and mastered relative to host links?
4. Is K-DST's ≈6/16 incoming talk-up rate related to host style, genre, intro availability, or compilation choices?
5. Does a station with shorter intros ever speak over an established vocal, or does the no-central-body rule survive?
6. Are plain artist/title announcements genuinely rare across the game, or just rare for these two personality-heavy hosts?
7. How does another station divide previous-song reaction, next-song setup, unrelated monologue, and world commentary?
8. Do stinger libraries repeat on a deterministic rotation, random draw, cooldown, or context rule?
9. Are caller rates lower in real play than in these asset-rich compilations?
10. How do commercial announcers, station imaging voices, callers, and hosts differ in width, EQ, compression, and loudness on one station that contains all four?
11. Does literal silence remain below one second per event on other stations, or can dead air become a deliberate character device?
12. How do real contemporary streaming/radio examples handle song starts when no reliable intro-duration metadata exists?

## Bottom line

### Measured

This 66:41 file contains 17 songs, 18 host-led windows, 16/16 host-mediated song boundaries, three produced calls, eleven stinger placements drawn from ten distinct designs, and no commercials. Median host-window length is 34.6 seconds. Approximate active voice occupies 11.3% of runtime. Only about six of sixteen incoming song launches definitely carry host speech, while at least nine outgoing tails do. All central song bodies remain uninterrupted. The host is close and strongly centered, stingers are wider and brighter, callers are narrower and band-limited, and the master is loud with modest dynamic range.

### Interpretation

K-DST demonstrates a different route to personality-driven radio than K-Rose. Its identity does not depend on reacting closely to every song or talking over nearly every intro. It depends on a stable host mythology, a repeatable stinger library, selective caller scenes, strong acoustic role separation, outgoing-tail continuity, and the confidence to finish talking before most records begin. For Zoya Radio, the key lesson is that a convincing station is governed by multiple independent clocks—**boundary opportunity, voice occupancy, semantic mode, overlap direction, imaging, calls, and silence**—not by one crude “how often should she comment?” knob.
