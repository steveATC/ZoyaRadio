# V-Rock production-pattern study for Zoya Radio

## Scope and result

This report analyzes the attached 77:22 V-Rock recording as a piece of radio production. It does not reproduce scripts, jokes, advertisements, song lyrics, characters, branding language, or other copyrighted creative expression. Spoken material is summarized only to classify timing, function, and acoustic treatment.

The central measured result is that V-Rock uses a more varied station clock than either supplied San Andreas reference. It does **not** put the DJ at every song boundary. Across 16 song-to-song boundaries:

- 12 contain the DJ;
- two are mediated only by short station imaging;
- two are direct song-to-song transitions.

Commercials add a separate clock: eight individual ads are arranged into six pods and occupy about 6:34 of complete produced-ad time, or 8.5% of the recording. Removing whole commercial pods leaves a 70:45 music/host/imaging/caller clock.

The dominant abstract grammar is not one universal link. V-Rock alternates among:

> compact DJ link → clean song launch

> DJ or imaging → one- or two-ad pod → optional return line → song

> imaging-only handoff → song

> two-song sweep → commercial reset → second two-song sweep

This is also a materially different source type from K-Rose and K-DST. The recording contains the fixed 2002 *Vice City* V-Rock sequence, rotated so that it begins with the imaging before *I Wanna Rock* and ends after *Yankee Rose*. Each of the 17 named songs appears once. The rotation is a property of this MP3 presentation, not evidence that an in-game listener always enters the loop at that point.

## Measurement method and confidence

### Direct measurements

- Source: `VROCK.mp3`, duration **77:22.236**, stereo MP3 at **32 kHz / 320 kb/s**.
- File metadata identifies the program as V-Rock from *Grand Theft Auto: Vice City* and dates it to 2002.
- The file was decoded and measured for integrated loudness, loudness range, true peak, silence, 200 ms energy, stereo width, spectral balance, and transition-level changes.
- Two local speech-recognition passes with word timing were used as navigation aids. Every station-voice and ad boundary was reconciled against speaker changes, acoustic changes, the program sequence, and the published V-Rock script order.
- Song, segment, and advertiser identities were cross-checked against published track and script references. External sources were not used to derive timing, loudness, overlap, or frequency statistics.
- The supplied K-Rose and K-DST reports were used to preserve comparable definitions where the presence of commercials permits it.

### Boundary confidence

- Clear DJ, caller, announcer, and ad-copy word boundaries: usually **±0.5 s**.
- Complete ad-module boundaries: usually **±1 s** because several ads continue with score or FX after the last spoken word.
- Clear song vocal boundaries: usually **±1 s**.
- Instrumental starts, fades, and mixed intro/outro sections: generally **±2–3 s**.
- The Autograph/Megadeth and Anthrax/Slayer direct boundaries are **±2 s** because there is no spoken marker and the records meet through instrumental material.
- Active-voice time is approximate. It was estimated only inside manually validated DJ/caller/imaging or commercial regions; sung vocals were excluded.

### Terms used below

- **DJ module:** one scripted appearance by the host, including deliberate pauses and any immediately embedded imaging or intro talk-up. A commercial can separate two DJ modules at the same song boundary.
- **DJ-bearing boundary scene:** the complete transition around one song boundary when the DJ appears. For the ad-excised statistic, commercial interiors are removed while intentional pauses and music inside the DJ scene remain.
- **Noncommercial station intervention:** an opening, boundary, or intro unit containing DJ speech and/or station imaging, excluding ads.
- **Produced imaging:** a prebuilt station ID, liner, sonic-logo unit, or guest-voice liner. A host merely saying the station name inside ordinary speech is not automatically a separate imaging placement.
- **Commercial unit/ad:** one self-contained fictional advertiser, advocacy, product, retail, event, or entertainment-promo segment.
- **Commercial pod:** one or more consecutive commercial units between the music/host portions of the station.
- **Active voice time:** approximate time occupied by DJ, caller, imaging, announcer, or commercial cast speech. Because speech can overlap music, it is not additive with song airtime.
- **Plain announcement:** artist/title identification with no joke, reaction, persona, station-world material, promo, or imaging.
- **Direct transition:** one named song proceeds to the next with no DJ, imaging, caller, or ad between them.

### Commercial scheduling observations — measured

- Commercials occur only at song boundaries.
- No commercial plays over an external music track and no established song body is interrupted for an ad.
- Pod size varies between one and two spots; the system does not force a fixed two-ad break.
- Five of six pods receive a post-ad DJ return. One deliberately returns straight to a song.
- Only half the pods use an explicit DJ verbal throw. Imaging is sufficient to mark the other entries.
- Pod starts are unevenly spaced. Linear gaps between starts range from about 4:06 to 21:48; treating the full sequence as circular creates an even longer gap across the file rotation.
- The eighth commercial is not merely inserted between random songs: it occupies the planned midpoint of a declared two-song → ads → two-song format.

## Host behavior — measured observations

### Frequency and restraint

- DJ speech appears at **12/16 song boundaries**, not every boundary.
- Imaging alone handles two additional boundaries.
- Two boundaries receive nothing but music, producing genuine two-song sweeps.
- The 16 DJ modules extrapolate to **13.6/h** only after commercial pods are removed; the more behaviorally meaningful DJ-bearing-boundary rate is **10.2/h**.
- About 63% of DJ modules are under 30 s. With calls removed, the median module is only 18.9 s.
- Active noncommercial station voice occupies about **10.8%** of the pod-excluded clock, close to K-DST and roughly half K-Rose.
- Every central song body is left alone.

### Placement relative to music

- One outgoing overlap is unambiguous: the DJ enters on the last Mötley Crüe tail, and side-channel music energy falls by about **9.3 dB** while total RMS changes by less than 1 dB.
- Several other DJ starts occur at a final chord, fade, rain/FX tail, or within about a second of the song end; source stems would be needed to distinguish every fade from a true voice-keyed duck.
- Approximately **five of sixteen incoming launches** contain definite station speech:
  - the DJ is already speaking as Mötley Crüe cues;
  - imaging overlaps the Ozzy opening;
  - a guest liner re-enters during the Loverboy intro;
  - the DJ re-enters about 26 s into the Autograph instrumental opening;
  - the DJ re-enters about 8 s into the Love Fist intro.
- The Yankee Rose launch is marginal because its opening effects and the last imaging syllable interleave tightly.
- None of these overlaps reaches an established verse/body.

### What the host talks about

Conservative hand coding across the 12 DJ-bearing song boundaries:

- Defensible previous-song, performer, pair, or title-theme relation: **about 3/12**.
- Discernible forward music reset or setup: **about 8/12**, most of them generic.
- Explicit next performer: **2/12**.
- Plain artist/title identification with no personality: **0/16 DJ modules**.
- Persona, station mythology, fictional-world material, promo/giveaway material, or caller conflict: present in **about 14/16 modules**. The remaining two are compact functional returns.

V-Rock is therefore less song-reactive than either K-Rose or K-DST. The DJ generally advances station identity, personal mythology, promotions, or fictional-world conflict. Music metadata is a rare service element rather than a default obligation.

### Callers, callbacks, and the hour-scale arc

Measured recurring devices include:

- A former-host dispute that establishes employment history and challenges the current DJ's knowledge.
- Two later callers who challenge the station's authenticity or music choices from different fictional-world positions.
- Repeated tension between the host and management/marketing.
- Repeated tests of what counts as authentic rock behavior, clothing, work, school, hair, or lifestyle.
- Repeated prize, event, merchandise, and weekend-promotion language.
- A recurring mascot grievance that appears in host speech and imaging and escalates near the end of the sequence.
- Repeated host insecurity disguised as self-aggrandizement.

The most important structural callback is the **four-song macro-block**. The host promises two records, a commercial interruption, and two more records. The station then fulfills that promise exactly:

1. Autograph;
2. direct transition to Megadeth;
3. imaging + one commercial + host reset;
4. Anthrax;
5. direct transition to Slayer;
6. host reaction to the completed pair.

That is measured track-spanning format behavior. It is not merely a series of independent comments that happen to be adjacent.

### Commercial heterogeneity

Commercials do not share one fixed acoustic preset. Across the eight complete units:

- median RMS ranges from approximately **−15.4 to −11.4 dBFS**;
- side-to-mid energy ranges from approximately **−28.7 to −15.1 dB**;
- stereo correlation ranges from approximately **0.943 to 0.997**.

Announcer-led advocacy or vehicle spots are relatively narrow. Cinematic trailers and dramatized product spots become wider through cast, score, ambience, and FX. The commercial layer is a family of production formats, not “one extra announcer voice over one universal bed.”

### Ducking, fades, and overlap

- The one definite outgoing DJ overlap reduces side-channel music energy by about **9.3 dB**, while centered voice keeps total RMS nearly unchanged.
- Across three clear DJ/incoming-intro samples, side-channel music during the host portion is about **2.3–12.4 dB** below the following clean music, with a median near **4.5 dB**.
- The Autograph talk-up is the strongest sampled intro ride; the Mötley Crüe and Love Fist overlaps are materially lighter.
- Imaging overlaps are still more arrangement-dependent. One is modestly attenuated; another sits over a naturally narrow intro and cannot support a clean fixed-duck estimate.
- Most incoming records launch cleanly. V-Rock uses dry breaks and reset points more often than K-Rose.
- Ads replace the music program rather than ducking an external song. Their internal beds, score, and FX are part of the pre-produced commercial scene.
- Hard editorial cuts and scene changes are more common inside commercials than between host and music, but long digital silence remains absent.

### Imaging, sonic logos, FX, and voices

- Approximately 16 produced imaging placements use a consistent deep male announcer distinct from the conversational DJ.
- Repeated sonic vocabulary includes distorted-guitar impacts, echoed station-name treatment, aggressive spoken positioning, mascot/bird effects, and compact rock stings.
- Imaging sometimes substitutes for the DJ entirely at a song boundary.
- The same imaging role participates in the station-co-branded concert promo, acoustically binding that commercial to V-Rock.
- Other ads use distinct announcers and character casts, preventing the DJ from sounding as if he personally endorses every fictional product.
- Caller voices occupy a narrow telephone space; host, imaging, callers, ad cast, and music are therefore legible before their words are fully processed.

## Recurring radio-format classification

These are abstractions of observed production units, not reusable scripts.

| Format | Observed structure | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Compact DJ reset | Song end → 7–25 s host unit → clean song launch | Common low-cost personality touch |
| Imaging-only handoff | Song → short produced liner/ID → song | Maintains identity while resting the host |
| Direct two-song sweep | Song A → no station element → Song B | Gives music an uninterrupted multi-track run |
| Tail talk-over | DJ enters on final song tail as music falls | Preserves momentum without touching the body |
| Delayed intro talk-up | Song begins → music breathes → DJ/liner re-enters → exits before vocal | Uses a long instrumental intro as scheduled space |
| Explicit ad throw | Host or imaging marks break → commercial pod | Makes the interruption feel intentional |
| Pod with return line | One/two ads → short DJ reset → clean music | Re-establishes host and station after a world change |
| Pod with direct music return | One/two ads → song | Avoids turning every break into a second host monologue |
| Cinematic commercial | Narrator + cast scene + score/FX + tag | Creates a separate fictional-world audio object |
| Station-co-branded promo | Imaging voice + event/music material + sponsor tags | Makes promotion feel native to station culture |
| Caller challenge | Host opens line → caller disputes identity/choice → host answers → music | Builds audience, conflict, and station history |
| Four-song macro-block | Promise → two-song sweep → ad midpoint → reset → two-song sweep → payoff | Sustains a format arc across multiple tracks |
| Recurring grievance callback | Short host/image references spaced across the hour | Creates continuity without requiring a serial plot in every link |

## V-Rock acoustic and sonic identity

### Observed

- Very loud, dynamically constrained overall master.
- Wide, bright hard-rock/metal records contrasted against centered DJ speech.
- A distinct, louder, wider imaging announcer with repeated guitar/echo/mascot vocabulary.
- Extremely narrow telephone callers.
- Commercials ranging from dry announcer-led spots to cinematic multi-character trailers.
- More clean song launches, imaging-only boundaries, and direct song sweeps than either supplied San Andreas station.
- Almost no literal dead air despite frequent hard scene changes.

### Interpretation

V-Rock's sonic identity is built from **hierarchy and theatrical contrast**:

1. songs occupy the widest, brightest plane;
2. the DJ occupies a close conversational center;
3. imaging expands outward and hits harder than the DJ;
4. callers collapse into a narrow outside line;
5. commercials temporarily replace the station with self-contained fictional scenes;
6. recurring guitar, echo, and mascot motifs pull those modules back under one station identity.

The host is important, but he is not required at every boundary. Imaging and scheduled music sweeps carry part of the identity load. That division of labor is why V-Rock can sustain personality while leaving 7-minute stretches free of station speech.

## Potential lessons for Zoya Radio — transfer hypotheses

These are research-derived possibilities, not approved implementation scope.

1. **Maintain separate station, DJ, caller, imaging, and commercial clocks.** One “speech frequency” number cannot describe this program.
2. **Exclude entire ad pods from host-cadence statistics.** Ad duration says nothing about how much the host talks.
3. **Schedule commercials only at safe song boundaries.** V-Rock never sacrifices an established song body to insert one.
4. **Let pod size vary.** Four single-ad pods and two doubles sound less mechanically uniform than a mandatory fixed count.
5. **Allow a direct-to-music return.** A commercial does not always require another host line before the next record.
6. **Use imaging as a substitute for the host.** Two boundaries remain branded while the DJ stays silent.
7. **Permit genuine music sweeps.** The longest station-voice-free span here is 7:37, yet the station remains recognizable when it returns.
8. **Treat multi-song formats as explicit scheduled objects.** The two-songs → ad → two-songs block is a planned scene spanning four records, not four unrelated generations.
9. **Prepare complex segments before their first audible beat.** Cast ads, FX, score, caller timing, and return logic have to be complete before playback to avoid latency holes.
10. **Separate commercial copy from commercial production.** A script, voice cast, bed, FX sequence, loudness target, and station transition are different layers.
11. **Use a small role palette consistently.** DJ, imaging announcer, caller line, trailer narrator, and ad cast each have distinct acoustic spaces.
12. **Do not force every ad into the same sonic template.** The eight spots vary by roughly 4 dB in median RMS and 14 dB in stereo-width measure while remaining inside one master.
13. **Preserve recognizable station motifs around heterogeneous content.** Imaging before or after a pod makes wildly different ads feel part of one station.
14. **Let some promotions relate to adjacent music.** A station-branded event spot can set up a relevant performer without requiring a literal DJ presell. This should be rare enough not to feel contrived.
15. **Use callers to challenge the host, not merely agree with her.** Conflict reveals personality and station history efficiently.
16. **Build hour-scale callbacks by compression.** Repeated management, authenticity, or mascot concerns become shorter and more legible over time.
17. **Count active voice separately from window length.** The 58-second Autograph window contains about 26 seconds of music with no speech.
18. **Use variable intro rides.** Measured incoming attenuation ranges widely; one fixed duck value would flatten arrangement differences.
19. **Keep music first.** The most transferable finding remains zero central-song interruptions, even in a station with commercials and a loud personality.
20. **Prefer pre-rendered local commercial assets for a local-first system.** Runtime selection can be procedural while expensive voice/music/FX production is completed and cached beforehand.

## What should not be generalized from this station

- **The exact eight-ad, six-pod clock.** It belongs to one fixed 2002 program loop.
- **A 75% DJ-boundary rate.** It is descriptive of this file, not a universal ideal.
- **The 17-song order or the MP3 start point.** The supplied file rotates the loop around a different point than the published script order.
- **The satire, advertisers, products, event, fictional bands, or commercial premises.** These are station-specific creative expression and should not be imitated.
- **The host's gender, age, insecurity, aggression, or subject matter.** Identity is not production law.
- **The low artist/title-announcement rate.** It may fit this character but would be unhelpful if copied blindly into a personalized station.
- **The availability of long instrumental rock intros.** Other genres may not support 8- or 26-second delayed talk-ups.
- **The −8.5 LUFS master.** This file may have been normalized or mastered differently from the supplied San Andreas recordings; copying it would be needlessly loud.
- **The ad mix as a contemporary loudness standard.** Modern streaming, accessibility, and device contexts differ from a 2002 game asset.
- **A fixed loop as the mature Zoya architecture.** Controlled repetition can build identity, but an adaptive station requires cooldowns, memory, safety validation, and anti-annoyance scheduling.
- **Caller rate.** A complete asset loop can make every available caller appear unusually frequent.
- **Commercials as mandatory.** They are one future fictional-station format, not a requirement for the current implementation or near-term roadmap.

## Bottom line

### Measured

This 77:22 recording contains 17 songs, 16 inter-song boundaries, 16 DJ modules, approximately 16 imaging placements, three produced calls, eight commercial units in six pods, and two direct song-to-song transitions. Commercial modules occupy about 6:34 or 8.5% of runtime. With pods removed, DJ-bearing boundaries occur about 10.2 times per hour and active station voice occupies about 10.8% of the remaining clock. The typical host module is short—19.6 seconds at the median—and all central song bodies remain uninterrupted. A planned four-song block produces two station-voice-free sweeps of about 7:37 and 6:59 around a commercial midpoint.

The DJ is close and centered; imaging is louder and wider; callers are quieter and almost perfectly mono; commercials occupy multiple distinct acoustic formats; and songs remain the widest, brightest layer. Commercials never interrupt song bodies and generally use a host or imaging transition at entry and/or return.

### Interpretation

V-Rock demonstrates that a convincing personality station can distribute identity across **host, imaging, callers, commercial worlds, promotions, callbacks, and music scheduling**. Its most useful lesson for Zoya Radio is not the quantity or style of the ads. It is the disciplined separation of clocks and roles: the host can stay out, imaging can carry the brand, commercials can become complete scenes, and a four-track format can unfold as a coherent arc while the songs still receive the overwhelming majority of attention.
